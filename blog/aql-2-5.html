AQL 2.5: How Pre-Shipment Inspection Actually Works

Updated October 18, 2026·All notes

"We do quality control" is the most useless sentence in international trade. AQL 2.5 is not — it is a number with a definition. AQL stands for Acceptable Quality Level: the worst percentage of defects you are willing to tolerate in a sampled lot, under a standardized sampling plan (the ISO 2859 series). AQL 2.5 is the industry-default level for general consumer goods, and it is the standard we run on every shipment.

Sampling, not counting every unit

Inspection is statistical. The inspector draws a sample from the finished lot — the sample size is set by the lot size and the inspection level, so a 500-piece order and a 5,000-piece order pull different samples — then counts defects against accept and reject thresholds. If the sample comes in at or under the accept number, the lot passes; beyond the reject number, it fails and the factory reworks before it ships. This is how a 5,000-piece drinkware order gets a real quality answer in an afternoon instead of a week of manual counting.

Defect classes matter more than the average

  • Critical — anything unsafe or non-compliant (a toy that fails an EN71 check, a bag with a sharp zipper edge). These are zero-tolerance and can fail the lot on their own.
  • Major — defects that would make you or your customer reject the item: a scuffed tumbler surface, a print misaligned on the branded panel, a bottle that leaks.
  • Minor — cosmetic nits that rarely affect the sale, judged within the AQL tolerance.

Where the inspection sits in our process

Quality work starts before production, not at the loading dock. Every factory in our network is pre-audited — capacity, certifications, export readiness — before we recommend it. During production we run inline inspections, so a problem on day three is a problem you hear about on day three, with photos. Then, before every shipment, the AQL 2.5 pre-shipment inspection produces a detailed report with photos that goes to you. Only after that report passes do we book the container.

Why the timing is the whole point

A quality problem found before booking is a rework conversation. The same problem found after the container is loaded is a demurrage bill, a re-inspection fee, a delayed sailing and a customer conversation you do not want to have. "Quality problems often surface after the container is already booked" is the single most expensive version of this failure mode — and the sequence above exists to keep every problem you do have in the cheap form.

Ask to see a report

When you evaluate any sourcing partner, ask for a recent pre-shipment inspection report — photos, sample size, defect counts, verdict. We share them with every quote. A partner who cannot produce one is telling you how they actually inspect.
