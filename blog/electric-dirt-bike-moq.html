Electric Dirt Bike MOQ: What One Unit and One Hundred Units Actually Cost

Updated October 27, 2026·All notes

An electric dirt bike MOQ is usually answered in one of two useless ways: a number with no price attached to it, or a price with no quantity attached. The number only becomes a decision when you put the floor, the tier and the fixed costs on the same page.

This note does that with the three models whose prices are published: the 72V Mimbob EM22-6, the 60V Nexvey Flash Pro and the 48V Mimbob TK5. Two of them can be ordered as a single unit. The third cannot, and the reason is more interesting than the number.

What an MOQ of 1 does and does not promise

A minimum order quantity of one means the factory will build a run of one. It does not mean that run costs the same per unit as a run of a hundred, and it does not mean freight suddenly becomes cheap. What it actually buys is information: a single unit in your hands, at your own inspection standard, before you commit working capital to a container.

That is worth paying for at the start and wasteful to keep paying for once you know the product sells. The rest of this note is about where that line sits.

Three models, three different floors

The floors are not a single company policy. They are published per model, and they are published next to the price, which is why they can be compared at all:

  • EM22-6 72V — MOQ 1. Price bands at 1–9, 10–49 and 50+ units.
  • Flash Pro 60V — MOQ 1. A single price across quantities rather than a ladder.
  • TK5 48V — MOQ 80. Price bands start at 80–99 and step again at 100+.

Anyone quoting you “our MOQ is 1” for a whole catalogue is describing the best case in that catalogue, not the model you asked about. The three priced models are listed together on the off-road category page, each with its own floor next to its own band.

The two thresholds that change the unit price

On the EM22-6 the unit price moves twice. At 10 units it drops from $2,199 to $1,550, which is $649 per unit, or 29.5 percent. At 50 units it drops again to $1,350, another $200, for a total of $849 per unit below the single-unit price — 38.6 percent.

On the TK5 there is only one step, at 100 units: $1,090 down to $1,010. That is $80 per unit, or 7.3 percent. The shape is different from the EM22-6 on purpose — a lower-priced product with a narrower band has less room to give.

What the unit price does not include

The gap between a good unit price and a good decision is the fixed cost sitting on top of it. Five of them matter here.

Branding and setup. Logo work, decals and colour changes are one-time work on a run. At one unit you carry all of it; at a hundred it is divided by a hundred. This is the single biggest reason a one-unit unit price looks high.

Inspection. AQL 2.5 pre-shipment inspection is a lot-level process with a photo report. The process does not shrink because the order did, so a one-unit order carries the same procedural weight on one bike.

Freight. Under FOB the main carriage is yours either way, but a single bike travels as its own shipment while a larger run shares one consolidated load out of Ningbo.

Cash exposure. One EM22-6 is $2,199. Eighty TK5 units are $87,200, committed before the first one sells. The unit price is lower, the risk is not.

Stock risk. A hundred units is a forecast. If the market disagrees with it, the loss is not in the unit price — it is in the inventory.

Why 80 units is a floor, not a punishment

The TK5's floor is easy to read as a supplier being difficult. Read it as arithmetic instead: the model sits at $1,090 per unit at 80 units, so the smallest published run is an $87,200 commitment. A factory that priced a 20-unit run off the same line would have to charge materially more per unit, and that price would look worse than the floor does.

The step from 80 to 100 is worth seeing in full. Per unit it falls $80, but the order grows by 20 units, so the total commitment rises from $87,200 to $101,000 — about $13,800 more. Those 20 extra units are not free; they are simply cheaper than the first eighty.

When one unit is the expensive way to buy

One unit stops being the right call the moment three things are true at once: the model is confirmed, the specification is locked, and the only open question is whether the market will absorb the volume. In that situation a single unit answers nothing new and pays the full fixed cost to do it.

It is also the wrong instrument when the buyer's real problem is price. If the target landed cost requires the 50-unit band, buying one unit is not a cautious first step, it is a different product at a different price.

When one unit is the right way to buy

One unit earns its premium in four situations: a new market with no sales history, a specification that has never been built before, a regulatory question that needs a physical sample, and a dealer who wants to show the product before committing.

In all four, the $2,199 is buying evidence, not inventory. The question to ask before ordering is not “is this expensive per unit” but “what will I know after it arrives that I do not know now”. If the answer is nothing, the single unit is a delay dressed as diligence.

How a one-unit order runs here

The process is the same as a container order, with smaller numbers at each step. You send the model, the destination and the quantity; you get a shortlist of three to five factories within 48 hours and a price for the band your quantity lands in, usually inside the seven-day sourcing cycle. Inspection at AQL 2.5 with a photo report happens before the container is booked, and the certificate for the model is verified against your order and sent to you, because certificates live with the factories rather than with us.

Buyers pay the factory price; our revenue is a commission from the factory after the order is placed, which is why a one-unit order is not a nuisance we price away. The same terms apply: FOB Ningbo, or CIF and DDP if you want one number at your door.

Questions buyers ask

Is there really a model with an MOQ of 1?

Yes. Two of the three priced models, the 72V EM22-6 and the 60V Flash Pro, are listed with an MOQ of 1 unit. The third, the 48V TK5, starts at 80 units, and that is stated in the same part of the listing as its price.

Why does the TK5 start at 80 units?

The listing publishes no price band below 80 units, so a smaller run cannot be quoted from a price list. Whether a run below 80 is possible at all is confirmed per order, not assumed on this page.

Can I combine models to reach a better price tier?

Each model's tiers are published per model. If you split a first order across two models, the quotation shows each model as its own line against its own tier, so you can see what each decision costs.

Does a single unit still get inspected?

Orders are inspected at AQL 2.5 with a photo report before the container is booked, and a one-unit order is the smallest case of that process. What changes with quantity is how many units the report covers, not whether it happens.

Are these prices landed, or FOB?

They are FOB Ningbo, per unit, at the quantity band shown, and subject to current quotation. Sea freight, destination charges, import duty and local homologation are separate; ask for CIF or DDP if you want one number at your door.
